Why It Matters

The significance and legacy of Treaty 3

What Makes Treaty 3 Different?

Treaty 3 stands apart from the other numbered treaties in several important ways. Unlike earlier treaties such as Treaty 1 and Treaty 2, the Anishinaabe negotiators refused to accept the Crown's initial offers and pushed back firmly until better terms were secured. This made Treaty 3 one of the most strongly negotiated treaties in Canadian history.

One of the most significant differences is the presence of outside promises — verbal commitments made by Crown representatives that were never included in the written treaty document. These included promises of medicine chests, famine relief, and protection from pestilence. While other treaties also had outside promises, Treaty 3 is particularly well documented in this regard, and the dispute over these promises has shaped the legal and political relationship between the Anishinaabe and the Crown for over 150 years.

Treaty 3 also covered a uniquely large and ecologically rich territory — approximately 55,000 square miles of land centred around the Lake of the Woods, one of the most important waterways in the region. This made the resource rights included in the treaty especially significant.

Stronger Negotiation

The Anishinaabe rejected the Crown's first offers multiple times, securing better reserve allocations and annuity payments than earlier treaties provided.

Outside Promises

Verbal commitments about medicine, famine relief, and pest control were made but never written down, creating lasting legal disputes.

Rich Territory

The treaty covered 55,000 square miles of forest and waterway, making hunting and fishing rights especially valuable to Anishinaabe communities.

Why Is Treaty 3 Historically Significant?

"We all live on Treaty land. Understanding the promises made — and broken — is the foundation of reconciliation."

Treaty 3 is historically significant because it set a precedent for how Indigenous peoples could negotiate with the Canadian Crown. The Anishinaabe demonstrated that organized resistance to unfair terms was possible and effective. Their success in securing better conditions influenced how later treaties were approached by both Indigenous nations and the Crown.

The treaty is also significant because it covered land that was critical to the construction of the Canadian Pacific Railway. Canada needed clear access through Anishinaabe territory to connect eastern and western Canada. This gave the Anishinaabe real negotiating power, which they used effectively.

Finally, Treaty 3 is significant because it remains a living document. It is not just a piece of history — its terms still have legal force today and continue to shape decisions about land use, resource extraction, and Indigenous rights across northwestern Ontario and southeastern Manitoba.

  • 1869–1870 The Anishinaabe block access through their territory, giving themselves leverage in negotiations with the Crown.
  • 1871 The Crown's first offer is rejected by Anishinaabe leaders who demand better terms.
  • 1873 Treaty 3 is signed at the Northwest Angle of Lake of the Woods after the Anishinaabe secure improved conditions.
  • 1990s–present Grand Council Treaty #3 leads ongoing negotiations over resource rights, land claims, and the fulfilment of outside promises.

The Legacy of Treaty 3 Today

The legacy of Treaty 3 is felt across many areas of life in northwestern Ontario and southeastern Manitoba. The Grand Council Treaty #3 continues to govern the political and cultural affairs of its 28 member First Nations, advocating for land rights, self-governance, and the honouring of treaty promises.

One important part of that legacy is the growing network of urban reserves. These are pieces of land near cities that have been granted reserve status, allowing First Nations to operate businesses and generate revenue while staying connected to their treaty rights. Urban reserves represent a creative and practical way for Treaty 3 communities to benefit economically from the treaty relationship.

Treaty 3 also plays a role in environmental stewardship. Because Anishinaabe peoples have constitutionally protected rights to hunt and fish on their traditional lands, they must be consulted before resource development takes place in the treaty area. This gives First Nations communities a meaningful voice in how the land and water of the Lake of the Woods region is managed and protected.

Most broadly, Treaty 3 reminds all Canadians that the land they live on is shared land — land that was never surrendered without promises in return. Honouring those promises is at the heart of reconciliation between Indigenous and non-Indigenous peoples in Canada today.
